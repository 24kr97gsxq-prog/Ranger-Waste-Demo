A dozen guesses baked into these screens

Every one of these was invented, not learned from Ranger. Any that are wrong are cheap to change now and expensive to change later.

Real in this demo, versus drawn

Actually works: assigning stops, advancing status, a customer request landing on the board, compactor hauls, tonnage capture, dry runs, English/Spanish, the map, and the CSV download — that file is real.
Placeholder: the addresses on the map are invented, nothing is reporting live GPS, every dollar figure is a Dallas market average rather than Ranger's own rate, and compactor fill percentages are invented. The brand colors, though, are Ranger's real ones.

  1. How compactors actually get serviced. They are in now as their own order type: the unit stays bolted down, the receiver box gets pulled and returned, no swap and no dwell clock. What is guessed is everything around that — whether hauls are scheduled, called in by the property, or triggered by a fill reading.
  2. Compactor fill percentage is reported, not sensed. The bars assume somebody at the property tells Ranger the unit is full. Real fill monitors are hardware on every unit plus a monthly fee each, the same shape of recurring cost as can GPS. Worth knowing whether Ranger already has them.
  3. Compactor pricing is blank on purpose. Haul, disposal and monthly rental all read "ask Ranger". Compactor deals are negotiated per site and there is no meaningful market average to borrow, so a gap is more honest than a number.
  4. The 15 yard has no published weight allowance. The 20 and 30 list 4 and 5 tons. The 15 lists none, so billing shows a gap instead of a number. Easy question, real hole.
  5. The 4 PM cutoff is treated as a hard line. The red bar counts down to it and anything requested after it is flagged next-day. In practice a dispatcher probably squeezes in a 4:20 call for a good customer. If so the rule needs an override, not just a flag.
  6. Brand colors are a placeholder. Set at the top of the file in four variables. Nothing was matched to their actual site — send the hex codes and it repaints.
  7. GPS on cans is assumed, not built. Trackers run roughly $50 to $200 of hardware per unit plus a monthly cellular fee on each one. On a 30-can fleet the subscriptions alone run into the hundreds per month, forever. Truck GPS is nearly free by comparison — the driver's phone already knows where it is.
  8. Spanish covers the app, not the content. Buttons and labels switch. The dispatcher's note about the gate code stays in whatever language it was typed in. Auto-translating job instructions is a good way to get a can set in the wrong place.
  9. One dispatcher, one board. If two or three people take orders at once, the board needs locking and a change log.
  10. A day at a time. No week view, no tomorrow's pre-builds. Real dispatch often works a rolling 10 days out.
  11. A driver's stops are ordered but not routed. Dispatcher decides sequence by hand. No mileage, no time windows honoured.
  12. Four order types — delivery, swap, pickup, relocate. Dump-and-return, live load, and dry run are missing and may matter more.
  13. The can number is known before the delivery. If drivers grab whatever is on the yard, the can gets assigned on the truck, not at the desk.
  14. Status is linear — dispatched, on site, complete. No blocked, no rescheduled, no customer-not-ready, no overweight.
  15. One photo and a note closes a stop. No weight ticket, no signature, no material contamination flag.
  16. Drivers always have signal. Nothing here works offline. If there are dead zones on their routes, this whole design changes.
  17. The customer text fires when the driver marks complete. Might be wrong moment. En route may be the one that actually saves phone calls.
  18. Customers get a link, not a login. No password, no account to manage, works for a site super who will never sign up for anything. If Ranger has property managers who need to see months of history across a dozen sites, that argument flips.
  19. Every request needs a dispatcher's yes. A customer asking for a pickup does not create work on a truck by itself. If Ranger would rather requests drop straight onto the board, that is a one-line change — but it hands route control to the customer.
  20. One contact per account. Real jobs have an office that pays and a super on site who actually needs the text. Those are often different people and different numbers.
Scope note, not a feature

Two very different QuickBooks exports

These get asked for in the same breath and they are not the same size of job. Which one Ranger needs depends on answers we do not have.

  1. A file they import by hand. We generate a CSV or IIF, someone in the office imports it into QuickBooks once a week. Cheap, boring, hard to break, and it never stops working because Intuit changed something. It is also manual forever.
    Customer,InvoiceDate,ItemName,Qty,Rate,Description Bluebonnet Roofing,2026-08-18,HAUL-30,1,,"Swap 30 yd, 9312 Skillman St, WO W-0391" Bluebonnet Roofing,2026-08-18,DISPOSAL-TON,4.2,,"Roofing, over 3 ton allowance"
    Rate column empty — no rate sheet
  2. A live sync through the QuickBooks Online API. Invoices appear in QuickBooks by themselves. Much nicer, and a real project: OAuth, matching our customers to theirs, mapping line items to their chart of accounts, sandbox testing, and token refresh that will eventually break at the worst moment.
There is a third-party gate on the live sync

Production access to QuickBooks Online is not just a key you generate. Intuit requires an app assessment questionnaire and reviews it before releasing production credentials — and that applies even when the app only ever talks to one company's books. Same species of delay as A2P 10DLC for texting: start it early or it stalls the launch.

And first: Desktop or Online? QuickBooks Desktop uses an entirely separate, older interface. If Ranger is on Desktop, option 2 above does not exist as described and option 1 is almost certainly the answer.

Scope note, not a feature

Emailing an invoice is a deliverability problem

Sending the message is the easy half. Getting it into an inbox is the half that decides whether Ranger gets paid.

  1. The sending domain has to be set up properly — SPF, DKIM and DMARC records on a domain Ranger controls. Invoices sent from a misconfigured domain land in spam, and nobody finds out until the receivables report looks wrong a month later.
  2. Invoices must be reproducible. If a customer disputes one, Ranger needs the exact PDF that was sent, not a regenerated one that may now render differently.
  3. Bounces have to be visible. A bounced invoice that nobody sees is an unpaid invoice.
  4. Someone has to press send. Automatically emailing money requests off the back of a driver tapping a button on a phone is how a wrong invoice reaches a customer before anyone in the office has looked at it. This wants a review step.

If QuickBooks is already emailing their invoices today, and it is, then the cheapest correct answer may be that we never email an invoice at all. We hand QuickBooks clean data and let it do the part it is already good at.
